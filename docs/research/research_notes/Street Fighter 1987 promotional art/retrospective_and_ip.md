# Street Fighter (1987): later official retrospective art, and IP/trademark questions for fan promotional art

Researched 2026-11-06. 13 tool calls. This is not legal advice; it summarises what sources say. Several primary sources (Capcom's own guidelines page, the Strong Museum record, the Street Fighter Fandom wiki) refused automated fetches (HTTP 403/402), so some points rest on press coverage of those pages. Each is marked.

## Which official compilations and books present the 1987 game's art, and are there later official illustrations of the 1987 cast?

### Takeaway
The 1987 arcade game is emulated in several Capcom compilations: Capcom Classics Collection Vol. 2 / Remixed, Capcom Arcade Hits Vol. 1, the Street Fighter 30th Anniversary Collection (2018) and Capcom Arcade 2nd Stadium (2022). Of these, the 30th Anniversary Collection's museum is the one confirmed to hold design documents and concept art, and Capcom PR says it covers the original game. Capcom Generation 5 is SF2-only and does not contain SF1. The best-known polished character illustrations of the full 1987 cast are later Bengus (CRMK) pieces reprinted in late-1990s/2001 Capcom art books such as Capcom Design Works. That attribution comes from a fan site and is uncertain.

The TurboGrafx-CD port *Fighting Street* was re-released on Wii Virtual Console in 2009, and Hi-Tech's MS-DOS version appeared in a *Street Fighter Series* CD-ROM collection — [Wikipedia: Street Fighter (video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))

No source found describes a Capcom Generation 5 gallery containing SF1 art.

### Inferences
- For a reference set of "official 1987 art," the most reliable items are the original flyers in the Arcade Flyer Archive and the in-game portraits. The 30th Anniversary museum is the most likely official modern reissue of SF1 design documents, but its SF1 contents need checking in-game or against a museum-content listing.
- The Bengus cast illustrations are later work, about 10 to 14 years after 1987, in a different style. A fan piece meant to evoke 1987 promotion should not use them as its model for "1987 look."

## Who are the credited artists for 1987-era Street Fighter art?

### Takeaway
Wikipedia credits Keiji Inafune with designing and illustrating the character portraits. The director was Takashi Nishiyama ("Piston Takashi") and the planner Hiroshi Matsumoto ("Finish Hiroshi"). The flyer illustrators are not credited in any source found, and the Strong Museum's c. 1987 design drawings are listed as "unidentified artist(s)."

### Cited Findings
- The staff list is Director Takashi Nishiyama (credited as "Piston Takashi"), Planner Hiroshi Matsumoto ("Finish Hiroshi"), Programmer Hiroshi Koike and Composer Yoshihiro Sakaguchi. Keiji Inafune designed and illustrated the character portraits, reportedly basing several designs on the manga *Karate Baka Ichidai* (citing IGN) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))
- Ken Hata of Atari Games designed the deluxe cabinet hardware in a joint venture — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))
- The c. 1987 design drawings at the Strong Museum are catalogued as "unidentified artist(s)" — [Strong Museum archive](https://archives.museumofplay.org/repositories/3/archival_objects/13316) (snippet only)
- Bengus (CRMK) is credited by a fan site with later SF1 cast illustrations from about 1998 to 2001 — [Fightersgeneration](https://fightersgeneration.com/games/sf-p6.html)

### Inferences
- The fan site's wording, that the "iconic" SF1 illustrations were by Bengus, can be misread as 1987 art. It is retrospective art. Inafune's credit covers the 1987 in-game portraits.

### Gaps
- No source found names the illustrators of the Japanese or US 1987 flyers, or the cabinet marquee and bezel art.

## Trademark/IP: word mark, logo, character names and likenesses, Capcom's fan-content guidelines, and how fan projects handle it

### Takeaway
Capcom holds US registrations and applications for the word mark STREET FIGHTER across several classes. I could not confirm a specific design-mark (logo) registration number in this pass. Capcom published official Fan Content Guidelines on 4 November 2025. They allow fan works without asking permission, within limits:
- no use of Capcom's company logo or the property's logo
- nothing that could be mistaken for official or approved content
- no direct copying of Capcom works without meaningful change
- sales only at hobbyist scale (doujin-like), and Capcom keeps the right to stop distribution

For an 1987-flyer pastiche, the title logo lettering, Capcom's logo, and copied flyer compositions are the clear no-go items.

### Cited Findings
- USPTO filings for STREET FIGHTER owned by Capcom include serial 75928157 (filed 12 Aug 1992; strategy books and pamphlets), serial 77386838 (filed 28 Feb 2008; computer game software) and serial 77462838 (filed 17 Jun 2008). STREET FIGHTER II has serial 74460210 (filed 1993) — [Justia trademarks: Capcom Co., Ltd.](https://trademarks.justia.com/owners/capcom-co-ltd-391635/page2) and aggregator pages (via search summary). Current status and classes should be verified in USPTO TSDR. No stylised-logo design-mark serial was found.
- A rumour that Namco owns the name is unconfirmed — [Nintendojo](https://www.nintendojo.com/?p=197145). In China, Capcom's application for the Chinese name of Street Fighter in class 41 was refused — [Mondaq](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/china/trademark/1368442/capcoms-application-for-the-chinese-name-of-street-fighter-in-class-41-was-refused-again). This shows registration coverage varies by country.
- Capcom's Fan Content Guidelines (official page: https://www.capcom-games.com/ja-jp/fan-content-guidelines/, which returned 403 to automated fetch) were announced in November 2025. As reported by Anime News Network:
  - Creators need not ask permission if they comply.
  - Sales are allowed only at a "hobbyist" scale (small volume, such as doujinshi), and Capcom will not define that scale precisely.
  - Profit-seeking sales are forbidden.
  - Garage kits may be sold at events such as Wonder Festival under those events' rules.
  - Prohibited content includes work that could be mistaken for official or approved Capcom content, work using Capcom's company logo or the property's logo, and direct copies of Capcom's copyrighted work without meaningful alteration. Also prohibited: third-party IP, content harmful to the property's image, and discriminatory, obscene, political or religious content.
  - Capcom may halt sale or distribution by contacting the creator or by legal means.

  [Anime News Network, 2025-11-05](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2025-11-05/capcom-publishes-guidelines-for-derivative-works/.230666)
- Other coverage says the guidelines cover illustrations, manga, games, novels, figures, music, cosplay and video. It also reports that they recommend a "fan-made" label and forbid direct use of official Capcom copyright notices. One report says mods are not mentioned — [Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/capcom-shared-game-fan-creation-and-derivatives-guidelines/); [Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/capcom-publishes-fan-content-guidelines-including-rules-against-offensive-fanart-and-commercial-use/); [EventHubs](https://eventhubs.com/news/2025/nov/07/capcom-fan-content-guidelines). Reports disagree on how lenient the commercial terms are, so check the official page.
- Capcom has a separate video policy (last updated 15 March 2021) for gameplay footage — [Capcom video policy](https://www.capcom.com/video-policy)
- Precedent of a fan game handled by Capcom: *Street Fighter X Mega Man* started as a fan game by Seow Zong Hui (as "Mega Man vs. Street Fighter," in development since 2009). It gained Capcom's support and approval and was released free from Capcom Unity on 17 December 2012. The developer had to pitch Capcom Japan and sort out the legal side — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_X_Mega_Man); [Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/how-capcom-got-involved-with-street-fighter-x-mega-man/)

### Inferences
- Practical reading of the guidelines for this project's fan promotional art:
  - Do not reproduce the STREET FIGHTER title-logo lettering, the Capcom corporate logo, or a ©CAPCOM notice. The logo ban is explicit, and an official-looking notice risks being "mistaken for official."
  - Label the piece as a fan work, with clear wording that it is not official or affiliated.
  - Do not trace or recompose an actual 1987 flyer, because that would be a direct copy without meaningful alteration.
  - Keep any distribution non-commercial.
- Character names and likenesses (Ryu, Ken, Sagat and so on) are part of Capcom's copyrighted work and, for the title, its trademarks. Under the guidelines, fan depictions are tolerated rather than licensed, and Capcom keeps takedown discretion.

### Gaps
- I could not read the official guidelines text directly (403), so the exact labeling wording and any explicit fan-game clause are unverified.

- No source was retrieved on how OpenBOR or M.U.G.E.N communities word their disclaimers. Fan-game disclaimer practice beyond SF X Mega Man is unsourced here. No Capcom cease-and-desist against a Street Fighter fan game was found in this pass. That means none was found, not that none exists.

## General principles: style vs specific compositions and logos; what "in the style of" means for an 80s arcade-flyer pastiche

### Takeaway
Legal commentators agree that copyright generally does not protect an artistic style as such. It protects specific finished works, and copying recognisable composition or expression can still infringe, as in *Steinberg v. Columbia Pictures*. Trademark adds a separate layer for logos and names that signal origin. That layer applies here regardless of style.

### Cited Findings
- Creative Commons says style is generally not protected by copyright, which attaches to specific works — [Creative Commons, 2023](https://creativecommons.org/2023/03/23/the-complex-world-of-style-copyright-and-generative-ai/)
- Duke's Christopher Buccafusco (2025) says federal courts that have considered style are split. He argues that style straddles idea and expression and proposes a new test for style-infringement claims — [Duke Law](https://law.duke.edu/news/professor-chris-buccafusco-proposes-new-way-evaluate-style-infringement-claims)
- *Steinberg v. Columbia Pictures* treated style as one ingredient of expression in a finding of substantial similarity — [Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2025/05/08/ghibli-ghiblification-copyright-and-style/)
- The Legal Artist says work imitating a style without copying specific pieces probably does not infringe. Fan art that reproduces characters or specific scenes reaches protected expression — [The Legal Artist](https://www.thelegalartist.com/blog/you-cant-copyright-style)

### Inferences
- For an 80s arcade-flyer pastiche, the generic period features are style:
  - airbrushed or painted figures
  - bold diagonal layout
  - halftone and limited-ink printing
  - sales-copy blocks
  - cabinet photo slot
  - spec bullets
- The things to avoid are 1987-specific:
  - an actual flyer's figure poses and arrangement
  - its exact copy text
  - the game's title logotype and Capcom marks
- Using the characters at all depends on Capcom's fan-content tolerance, not on the style principle.
- Trademark-law treatment of logo imitation in fan art (likelihood of confusion, nominative use) was not covered by the sources retrieved. The guidelines' explicit logo ban makes it moot for this project.

### Gaps
- No authoritative trademark-law explainer on fan use of game logos was retrieved. Jurisdiction differences (Japan vs US) were not covered.
