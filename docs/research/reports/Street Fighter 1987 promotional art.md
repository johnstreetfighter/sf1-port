# Flames, landmarks and cartoons sold Street Fighter

The original 1987 *Street Fighter* was sold with two visual languages, and a fan illustrator can borrow both without copying a single piece. Capcom's English export flyer, printed in Japan, has a hand-painted cover. A red-haired Ryu in a white gi flies through orange-yellow flames over a pagoda roof, and torn paper opens onto Mount Rushmore. The back page and the US cabinet art (side panel, marquee, control panels) use flat cel-cartoon fighters with black ink outlines, set on stripes of magenta, red, orange and yellow. The 1988-89 home ports added two more looks: Hudson's painted PC Engine cover of Ryu before a flaming Mount Rushmore, and U.S. Gold/Go!'s painted night-alley gang scene. Almost none of this art is credited. Keiji Inafune is credited with the in-game portraits, and the PC Engine cover painting is attributed to Takayuki "Ryukow" Masuo by a single fan source. The flyer painters and the cabinet illustrators are unknown. The Japanese domestic flyers (チラシ) and cabinet art could not be found or viewed in this research, so the visual analysis below rests on Western and export material. The style is free to borrow: hot flame gradients against cool blues, world landmarks, flying kicks, tilted screenshot cards and condensed italic sales lettering. The specific marks are not. Capcom's Fan Content Guidelines of November 2025 explicitly forbid using the property's logo or Capcom's logo, work that could be mistaken for official content, and direct copies without meaningful change. For *Street Fighter: The World Tournament*, that rules out the flame-brush STREET FIGHTER logotype, the CAPCOM box, any ©CAPCOM notice, and the recognisable layouts of the flyers and box covers.

## The surviving record is mostly Western, and nearly all of it is uncredited

The inventory below is everything the research could place, with the medium and look described from direct viewing of the scans where possible. Japan sold two products: the pneumatic **デラックスアップライト** (Deluxe Upright) from late August 1987 (Famitsu gives **30 August 1987**), and the six-button **テーブル スタンダードタイプ** (table, standard type) from late October 1987. The table version topped Game Machine's table chart ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ストリートファイター_(ゲーム)); [Famitsu](https://www.famitsu.com/news/202208/30273592.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))). Each Japanese product probably had its own flyer, but no Japan-flagged scan turned up on the Arcade Flyer Archive (TAFA), and those flyers' imagery, palette and lettering are **unverified**. The Japanese cabinet does appear in one UK flyer's photograph. It is a dedicated upright with a painted marquee showing a fighter and the flame logo, a red curved CAPCOM control-panel fascia, and painted side art of a fighter ([TAFA 4009](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/4/street-fighter-04004-01.jpg)).

The deluxe cabinet's own topper was not the flame logo. Flyer photos show STREET FIGHTER in blue and white outlined block letters with small figures, though only at small size ([TAFA 4390 back](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/4/street-fighter-1943-the-battle-of-midway-bionic-co-04385-02.jpg)). Atari Games built that cabinet. Ken Hata of Atari is credited with the deluxe hardware ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))), but no source says who drew the cabinet graphics. No standalone bezel art was found. Trade-press ads (RePlay, Play Meter, Cash Box) and Japanese magazine art (Gamest, Beep!, Game Machine) were **not examined**. The magazine record that does exist is ranking data: 6th for Best Graphics at the 1st Gamest Award, and #3 on Game Machine's upright chart in October 1987 ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ストリートファイター_(ゲーム)); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))).

On character art, the in-game credits list six pseudonymous character designers. English Wikipedia credits **Keiji Inafune** with the character portraits, several of them based on the manga *Karate Baka Ichidai* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game)); [Wikipedia: Inafune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Inafune)). The polished paintings of the SF1 cast that circulate today are by **Bengus (CRMK)**. A fan site dates them to after 1987, popularised through art books around 1998-2001 ([Fighters Generation](https://www.fightersgeneration.com/games/sf-p6.html)). They are anachronistic as a model for a "1987 look". The Strong National Museum of Play holds "Street Fighter design drawings, unidentified artist(s), c. 1987" ([Strong Museum](https://archives.museumofplay.org/repositories/3/archival_objects/13316); seen only as a search snippet).

## Painted grit up front, cel cartoons in the cabinet

The Japan-printed cover is the closest thing to an official 1987 key visual. Its method is painterly realism with visible brushwork, and the figures look like manga fighters rather than sprites. A diagonal flying kick drives the top of the frame, and flame wraps the hero. A collage of landmarks (Japanese temple, American monument) is joined by torn-paper edges, which sells a "world tour" before the reader sees a single rule. The sales block sits underneath: a cutout cabinet, real screenshots and a yellow features panel ([TAFA 1037](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/1/street-fighter-01037-01.jpg)). Electrocoin's UK flyers show how strong this painting was as an asset. They had no new illustration commissioned and simply recropped Capcom's Ryu and flames under their own catalogue typography ([TAFA 4007](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/4/street-fighter-04002-01.jpg); [4008](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/4/street-fighter-04003-01.jpg)).

The US cabinet package speaks a second, cheerier language. The cartoon fighters have flat fills and black keylines, set on banded "rainbow" speed stripes, and each control panel stages a duel between two cartoon characters. The colour coding is not loyal to the sprites. Ryu wears blue on the side art and marquee and teal on the pad overlay, but white in-game and in the flyers ([KLOV 48898](https://www.arcade-museum.com/images-game/48/street-fighter-48898.jpg); [48906](https://www.arcade-museum.com/images-game/48/street-fighter-48906.jpg)). That points to an illustrator working from a palette brief rather than from the game. The flyer back also explains the controls with comic devices: the fist-on-pad "Bang!", pill-shaped section labels, and a country-tag map with a "?" over the hidden bosses ([TAFA 1037 back](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/1/street-fighter-01037-02.jpg)). Its advice to strike the pads with a closed fist contradicts Capcom staff and Gamest, who said to use an open palm ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(video_game))). That is a useful reminder that these flyers sold excitement, not accuracy.

## Home ports traded the dojo for night streets and Rushmore

Hudson Soft's *Fighting Street* (PC Engine CD-ROM², 1988, HCD8002) has a square white insert holding a large circle. Inside it, a waist-up Ryu with orange-red curly hair raises both fists before a flame-washed Mount Rushmore, with small rival figures at the sides and a blue-white energy streak. The jagged red-to-yellow dry-brush title echoes the arcade logo and breaks out of the circle's bottom edge ([TheGamesDB front](https://cdn.thegamesdb.net/images/original/boxart/front/93991-1.jpg); [back](https://cdn.thegamesdb.net/images/original/boxart/back/93991-1.jpg)). vgdensetsu.net credits the painting to **Takayuki "Ryukow" Masuo**, citing the artist's own tweet; this is uncorroborated ([vgdensetsu.net](https://vgdensetsu.net/?p=1585)). NEC's 1989 TurboGrafx-CD release (TGXCD1001) cropped the same painting into its white house frame, with arched gold capitals instead of the brush lettering ([VGCollect](https://vgcollect.com/item/13982)). GamesRadar later listed it among the series' awful box art ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/awful-box-art-street-fighter-series)).

Europe's Tiertex conversions (Go!/U.S. Gold, 1988) used one uncredited painting across all formats. In a wet night alley, a long-haired hero seen from behind wears a leather jacket with STREET FIGHTER daubed on it and faces six street toughs. It sits under the arcade logo, on purple, magenta and blue bands, with a red CAPCOM box and the line "Giants of the video games industry" ([Spectrum Computing inlay](https://spectrumcomputing.co.uk/zxdb/sinclair/entries/0009425/StreetFighter.jpg)). The same art carried a two-page and a single-page magazine ad: an italic all-caps "....NOTHING STANDS IN YOUR WAY" headline, labelled screenshots for each format, review quotes and a price grid. ZXDB indexes **25 placements** from July 1988 to March 1989 across ACE, C&VG, Crash, Sinclair User, The Games Machine, Your Sinclair and Popular Computing Weekly ([Spectrum Computing entry 9425](https://spectrumcomputing.co.uk/entry/9425); [ad scan](https://spectrumcomputing.co.uk/pub/sinclair/games-adverts/s/StreetFighter_2.jpg)). The same painting was reused for budget re-releases through about 1991: Erbe in Spain, and Kixx with a pink lattice and zigzag frame ([Kixx inlay](https://spectrumcomputing.co.uk/zxdb/sinclair/entries/0009425/StreetFighter(Kixx).jpg)). Capcom USA's own Amiga box went the other way: a backlit gi silhouette in a smoky doorway on black, distressed yellow capitals, and the tagline "So you think you're mean enough?" ([LaunchBox](https://gamesdb.launchbox-app.com/games/details/73254-street-fighter)). The covers of the MS-DOS version and the compilations were not seen, and who developed the DOS port is disputed between sources.

Later official presentation of the 1987 game comes mostly through emulation. *Street Fighter 30th Anniversary Collection* (2018) has a museum of concept art and design documents that Capcom PR says covers the original game, though which SF1 items it holds is unconfirmed. *Capcom Arcade 2nd Stadium* (2022) includes the arcade ROM ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/street-fighter-was-almost-released-for-the-8-bit-nes-1826405783); [Wikipedia: Capcom Arcade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Capcom_Arcade_Stadium)). Art books such as UDON's *The Art of Street Fighter* (2021, 448 pp.) cover the SF1 era, but which 1987 pieces they reprint was not checked ([Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.ca/books/The-Art-of-Street-Fighter-Hardcover-Edition/Capcom/9781772941609)).

## A style kit for The World Tournament's banner and post images

Pulled together, the advertising has a recognisable grammar that belongs to the period rather than to any one piece. Copyright protects specific works and their recognisable composition, not a style as such ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/2023/03/23/the-complex-world-of-style-copyright-and-generative-ai/); [Authors Alliance on *Steinberg*](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2025/05/08/ghibli-ghiblification-copyright-and-style/)). Courts are split on how far style goes ([Duke Law](https://law.duke.edu/news/professor-chris-buccafusco-proposes-new-way-evaluate-style-infringement-claims)), so the safe course is to recombine the ingredients freshly. The table sorts what is period style, free to use, from what is specific to these pieces.

| Element | 1987-88 practice | How a fan piece can use it |
|---|---|---|
| Technique | Opaque gouache/acrylic painting with visible brushwork for key art; flat cel fills with black keylines for cabinet and explainer art | Pick one register per image: a painterly hero image for the README banner, and cel-cartoon figures for explainer or forum graphics |
| Composition | A strong diagonal action pose (flying kick, lunge) over a landmark backdrop; art on top, a sales block below (screenshots, feature panel) | Use your own pose and a landmark the flyers did not use (Great Wall, Big Ben, a Thai temple, a Chinatown street; the game has Great Wall and Chinatown stages, per [TAFA 4009](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/4/street-fighter-04004-01.jpg)). Avoid Mount Rushmore together with a pagoda, or with torn paper |
| Palette | Flame gradient of red to orange to yellow against cool blue or teal; stripe bands of magenta, red, orange and yellow on black; UK ports in night violet and blue | A warm/cool split works. Change the stripe order, angle or count so it does not read as the marquee |
| Typography | Condensed italic grotesque headlines; white casual brush script with drop shadow; rounded-pill section labels; spot-colour tables | Set your own title in a condensed italic face, or a hand-lettered brush that is clearly not the SF flame mark |
| Motifs | World map with country tags; a "?" over hidden bosses; screenshots tilted in black monitor bezels; a cutout cabinet; starbursts; sound words like "Bang!"; a features panel | Use real screenshots from the fan port in tilted bezels; a tagged world map of your own tour; a starburst reading "FAN-MADE" |
| Copy voice | Hard-sell superlatives ("most sensational", "most amazing") and challenge lines | Write new lines. Do not reuse "Let nothing stand in your way", "So you think you're mean enough?" or "packs a lot of punch" |

## What to leave alone: the flame logo, Capcom marks and the five famous layouts

Capcom's **Fan Content Guidelines (4 November 2025)** let fans make work without asking, within limits. They prohibit using Capcom's company logo or the property's logo, content that could be mistaken for official or approved, and direct copies without meaningful alteration. Sales are allowed only at a hobbyist scale, and Capcom may halt distribution ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2025-11-05/capcom-publishes-guidelines-for-derivative-works/.230666)). Coverage also reports that the guidelines recommend a "fan-made" label and bar the use of official copyright notices ([Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/capcom-shared-game-fan-creation-and-derivatives-guidelines/); [Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/capcom-publishes-fan-content-guidelines-including-rules-against-offensive-fanart-and-commercial-use/)). Capcom's own page refused automated fetches, so check it directly ([capcom-games.com](https://www.capcom-games.com/ja-jp/fan-content-guidelines/)). Capcom also holds US filings on the STREET FIGHTER word mark (for example serial 77386838, for computer game software) ([Justia](https://trademarks.justia.com/owners/capcom-co-ltd-391635/page2)). No registration for the stylised logo was confirmed, but the guidelines ban the logo anyway.

The project's own title contains the STREET FIGHTER word mark. Writing the name in plain text alongside a clear "fan-made, not affiliated with Capcom" label is the minimum (this is my reading, not legal advice). Setting those words in the flame-brush logotype is not acceptable, and neither are the US marquee's flat redraw, Electrocoin's pink version, or the PC Engine echo of it. Any jagged red-to-yellow dry-brush wordmark with a black drop shadow will read as the property logo. The same goes for the CAPCOM wordmark, the red outlined CAPCOM box, "Giants of the video games industry" with its purple striped band, and any "TM © CAPCOM" line. These are the marks most likely to make a fan banner look official.

Five compositions are distinctive enough that echoing them would count as copying:

- Ryu's flaming flying kick over a pagoda, with torn paper revealing Mount Rushmore (flyer A)
- Ken and Ryu at the centre of a map of country-tagged opponent cards, with a "?" boss card (flyer A's back)
- Waist-up Ryu in a circle before a flaming Rushmore, with the title breaking out of the circle (Masuo/Hudson)
- The leather-jacketed hero seen from behind, facing a gang in an alley (Go!)
- Stacked Ken and Ryu on a rainbow arc (US side art)

Character likenesses are a separate matter. Drawing Ryu, Ken or Sagat at all relies on Capcom tolerating fan works, not on the style principle, and Capcom keeps the right to take work down ([The Legal Artist](https://www.thelegalartist.com/blog/you-cant-copyright-style)). The one precedent found is *Street Fighter X Mega Man*, which began as a fan game and reached release only after the developer pitched Capcom Japan ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/how-capcom-got-involved-with-street-fighter-x-mega-man/)).

## Conclusion

The 1987 campaign never settled on one look. Capcom Japan painted grit and flame, Capcom USA dressed cabinets in candy-striped cartoons, and Europe's publishers invented a leather-jacket street brawler unrelated to the game. That inconsistency frees the fan port. "1987 Street Fighter advertising" is a set of habits (warm flame against cool night, landmark collage, diagonal action, real screenshots in tilted bezels, condensed italic hard-sell copy) rather than a house style tied to one owner. The protected part is narrow and easy to name: the brush logotype and its redraws, Capcom's marks and notices, and five specific layouts. A banner that pairs an original pose with an unused landmark, original lettering, the port's own screenshots and a visible fan-made label sits squarely in the period without borrowing anything Capcom owns outright.

The biggest open gap is Japan. The domestic chirashi for the Deluxe Upright and the table version, the Japanese cabinet art up close, and contemporary Gamest and Game Machine ad art were never viewed. If the fan art aims to look "Japanese 1987" rather than "export 1987", check Yahoo! Auctions or Mandarake listings, onitama.tv's Game Machine scans and the 30th Anniversary Collection museum before you commit to a palette.
